# Privacy, Terms and refund pages: the strongest honest use (12 Oct 2026)

**Status: research. The corrections it produced are applied to `2026-10-12-disclosure-placement-plan.md` (its §9). Nothing in `src/` changed. The plan stays on hold until the founder approves its §6.**

**Where it came from.** Two read-only research agents run in parallel on 12 Oct 2026 (150 tool calls, primary pages only): one on how respected companies split their public and pricing pages from their Terms, Privacy and refund pages; one on what the law and Paddle require at the point of sale versus what may live only in the Terms. Both read the placement plan first and checked it line by line. Tags: **V** verified against a page or file line named beside it, **E** judgement, **U** unverified.

**The founder's idea being checked (12 Oct 2026):** show the "don't" lines that make a positive impact; put the negative parts in the Terms and Privacy pages, which users rarely read but tick; use definitions the way big marketplaces do ("no expiry" on the home page, a twenty-appeal limit in the Terms); this is the globally recognised way of using these pages.

---

## 0. The short answer

- **Half of the premise is right, and it is the half the plan already used.** Definitions (what a case, a round, a device is), operating rules (rate limits, fair use, support hours) and privacy mechanics belong in the Terms and Privacy pages. The Terms tick is what makes them a contract (V, Berman v. Freedom Financial, 9th Cir. 2022: conspicuous notice plus an affirmative act).
- **The other half is the thing regulators fine.** A limitation that would change the decision to pay cannot live only in the Terms. The US regulator's own words: "Written disclosures or fine print may be insufficient to correct a misleading representation" (FTC, 1983); "Necessary disclosures should not be relegated to 'terms of use' and similar contractual agreements" and a disclosure "cannot cure a false claim" (FTC, 2013); "burying key limitations of the product or service in dense Terms of Service documents that consumers don't see before purchase" is a named dark pattern (FTC staff report, Sept 2022). UK law since 6 Apr 2025 says the same in statute: omitting includes giving information "in such a way that the consumer is unlikely to see it" (DMCC Act 2024 s.227(4)); CMA fines up to 10% of turnover. Pakistan's own competition regulator says it too: "The use of fine print disclaimers and qualifications that are difficult to read and/or hide important information must be avoided" (CCP guidelines, 3 Apr 2023); its deceptive-marketing fines reached Rs1.007bn in the year to July 2025 (all V).
- **The best companies do exactly what the plan proposes, with one difference the plan now matches.** Stripe, Mercury, Wise, Shopify, Notion and Linear put the qualifier beside the headline number on the pricing page itself ("Unlimited file uploads" + "~5GB max per file"; "$0/mo" + "Mercury is a fintech company, not an FDIC-insured bank" at the top of the pricing body). What goes to their Terms is definitions, behaviour-based fair use with no number (Wise: rate shopping, excessive cancellations, "usually temporary"), and legal-process clauses. **No peer checked hides a numeric cap behind a public "unlimited" or "every"** (V). So the twenty-response cap stays, but as an anti-abuse rule about one seller's own case, with the number as an outer bound and never on a public page.
- **The three lines stay at the point of sale** (Amazon decides; software, not a law firm; some cases not sold). Mercury keeps its status disclaimer on its pricing page and does not read weak; Riverbend makes no success claim and needs no footnote; Thompson & Holt claims "100% Success*" high on the page and buries "No company can guarantee" in © 2020 footer small print, which is the shape the FTC report describes (V). AppealDeck claims no number, which is the stronger position.
- **Four things were wrong or missing in this morning's plan, now fixed in it:** the "faster than a law firm" rewording (a comparison with lawyers, the DoNotPay category; struck); "Paddle reads the whole site" (Paddle's pages say it checks that the pages exist, are linked from navigation and the product fits its policy, and "automatically approve[s] most domain submissions"; the reason is corrected); the files line that dropped the loss sentence; and the Terms lack things Paddle requires (the Merchant-of-Record sentence) or the law expects (a governing-law clause that admits the purchase is under Paddle's Buyer Terms, a changes clause that promises email, a conspicuous liability cap, a Terms tick at checkout).
- **Two Paddle findings for the founder (section 4):** Paddle's published seller-policy guidance says a change-of-mind refund window "must be 14–90 days" and "a sub-14-day refund window ... fail[s] review" (V, verified from this machine). The live site promises 7 days (D8). And the checkout today has only the EU-withdrawal checkbox; there is no "I have read the Terms and the Refund policy" control, so every Terms clause the plan relies on is weakly accepted (V, `ConsentRow.tsx`).

**Two facts the plan did not use:**

- **The Pass buyer is a trader, not a consumer**, under the EU and UK definitions (CRD art. 2(1): a consumer acts "outside his trade, business, craft or profession"; CRA 2015 s.2(3): "wholly or mainly outside"). A suspended seller buying a tool to get a selling account back is acting for trade. So for the Pass as sold today (EU/UK blocked, US buyers) the binding standards are the FTC Act and state law; the EU/UK consumer directives are the right analogy for wording, not the enforcement route (E, high confidence). The D8 checkbox does no harm to a trader and matches Paddle's checkout logic for consumers, so it stays.
- **The purchase contract is with Paddle under Paddle's Buyer Terms** (English law; New York law for US consumers), not with AppealDeck under Pakistani law. AppealDeck's "laws of Pakistan" clause can govern only the licence and use, and should say so (V, Paddle Buyer Terms 31 Mar 2026).

**The Pakistan exposure, plainly.** The Competition Act 2010 s.10 bans "deceptive marketing practices", including "false or misleading information to consumers ... related to the price, character, ... properties, suitability for use, or quality" (V); the CCP's 2023 guidelines add that silence or omission can deceive (§4.7.1), that every representation needs "independent and recognizable substantiation ... prior to marketing" (§4.8.1), that fine-print disclaimers hiding important information "must be avoided" (§6.3), and that disclosures must be "clearly and conspicuously", "prominently/instantly visible", "appropriately placed" (§6.5). Penalty: up to Rs75 million or 10% of annual turnover (s.38). FY2024-25: 12 orders, Rs1.007bn, 8 of them deceptive marketing (V, CCP release 14 Jul 2025). The realistic route to AppealDeck is a competitor complaint, not a US buyer (E).

## 4. Four things the Terms and checkout are missing (found by the law agent; two re-verified here)

1. **The Merchant-of-Record sentence.** Paddle's seller-policy guidance requires the Terms to state that Paddle is the reseller and Merchant of Record, for example "Our order process is conducted by our online reseller Paddle.com. Paddle.com is the Merchant of Record for all our orders." (V, re-verified 12 Oct 2026: the document says "Sellers using Paddle as merchant of record must publicly publish three documents"; it is not limited to embedded-billing partners.) `legal.ts` has no such sentence in the Terms (Paddle appears only in Privacy, the consent note and the checkout note).
2. **The refund window.** The same guidance: a change-of-mind window "must be 14–90 days (a 30-day money-back guarantee is a good default)"; "never 'all sales are final' or 'no refunds'; Paddle rejects these"; and under common pitfalls, "'All sales are final' or a sub-14-day refund window — both fail review." (V, re-verified.) The live site promises 7 days (D8; `legal.ts:260`). Paddle's general help page sets no number, so whether this applies to a direct seller is the founder's to confirm with Paddle; raising 7 to 14 costs nothing measurable and matches Paddle's own 14-day refund-request window. **Founder decision (F-15).**
3. **Governing law.** The sale is with Paddle under English law (New York law for US consumers). The Terms say Pakistani law governs "any dispute"; that is wrong for the purchase and will not displace a US regulator or a chargeback. The clause can govern the licence only.
4. **A Terms tick at checkout.** The only consent control is the EU-withdrawal checkbox (`ConsentRow.tsx:25` renders `LEGAL.consent.withdrawalCheckbox.label`; `PurchasePanel.tsx:173`; the Terms link is only in the footer). Under Berman, every Terms clause the plan relies on (definitions, fair use, the liability cap, governing law) binds only with conspicuous notice and an unambiguous act. Add "I have read the Terms and the Refund policy" with links to the consent row's label (V, re-verified by grep).

Also: the changes clause ("posted here and, where they affect you directly, emailed") should promise email to every account holder before a change takes effect and never reduce a Pass already bought (Douglas v. Talk America, 9th Cir. 2007: posting without notice binds nobody); the as-is and liability-cap paragraph is plain body text and should be conspicuous with a "does not apply to fraud" carve-out; and a one-line "AppealDeck is a business tool for Amazon sellers; by buying a Pass you confirm you buy it for your selling business" records the trader status the EU/UK definitions turn on.
