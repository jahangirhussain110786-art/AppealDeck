# Agency outreach email + ten-seller conversation script

21 September 2026. Two ready-to-use drafts for the next-30-days plan from the commercial review (`2026-09-21-commercial-review.md`). Nothing here has been sent or scheduled — both are drafts for the founder to review, edit in their own voice, and send themselves. I do not send messages on your behalf without your explicit go-ahead each time.

---

## Part 1 — Agency/consultant outreach email (~20 sends, one week)

### Who to send it to

Public, findable, already-reachable appeal consultants and small agencies — not the giants (ecommerceChris, Riverbend, AMZ Sellers Attorney) who have no reason to adopt anyone else's tooling. Target the tier below them: smaller shops, solo consultants, VA-team operators who currently manage client cases by hand or in spreadsheets. Sources already in `Planning/07-REFERENCE/02-COMPETITOR-DOSSIER.md` and the saved agency research are a starting list; a public "contact us" or a named consultant's own email is fine — no scraping, no bulk/cold-email tooling, no misrepresentation of who you are.

### The email

> **Subject: A question about how you track appeal cases**
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm building a tool for Amazon sellers who need to respond to a suspension or policy notice — not another appeal generator, but a case record: what Amazon asked for, what evidence was gathered, what was sent, and what came back, all in one place with encrypted local storage.
>
> I'm not selling anything today. I'm trying to understand how consultants like you currently keep track of a case across weeks and multiple rounds with Amazon — spreadsheet, email folders, a paid tool, or something else — and whether a shared case record (something you could hand to a client, or reference on the third round of correspondence) would be useful in your work.
>
> If you have 15 minutes this week, I'd like to ask a few questions and, if it's relevant, show you what I've built. If not, even a one-line reply on how you currently track cases would genuinely help.
>
> Thank you,
> [Your name]
> AppealDeck — [link]

**Why this wording:** it asks a question before pitching, states plainly it isn't a sales email today, and names the actual differentiator (the case record) instead of "AI appeal writer" — which most of them already sell in-house. It also gives an easy low-effort reply path (one line) so a busy consultant with no time for a call still gives you a data point.

### The five questions, if they say yes to a call

1. Walk me through how you track one case today, from intake to close.
2. What's the most annoying part of managing a case that goes through two or three rounds with Amazon?
3. If a client came to you having already used something like this — an organized case record with the evidence and history in one place — would that save you time, or would you rather start from your own intake?
4. Is there a price where this would be a routine tool you'd use across clients, versus one you'd only try for free?
5. What would make you trust handing a client's account details to a tool you didn't build?

### What a "yes" looks like

Not a signed contract — a concrete answer to Q4 with a number attached, or an unprompted "I'd pay for that" about a specific feature. Vague enthusiasm ("interesting, keep me posted") is not a signal; a stated price or a request to try it with a real client case is.

## Part 2 — Ten real seller conversations (unpaid, this week)

### Who to talk to

Sellers who have been through at least one round with Amazon — decoded, submitted, or currently waiting — found through the free decoder's own users if any exist yet, or through participating (never promoting — the forums ban it) in Seller Central forums, ASGTG, or similar spaces where suspended sellers already discuss their situation. The goal is ten real conversations, not ten survey responses; a synchronous call or a real back-and-forth thread beats a one-shot form.

### Opening line

> "I'm building a tool for sellers dealing with a suspension or policy notice, and before I build more of it, I want to understand what's actually been hardest for you. Would you be willing to talk through your situation for 10–15 minutes? I'm not selling anything — I just want to learn."

### The question set

Ask in this order. Let them talk; don't lead them to the answer you want.

1. **What happened, in your own words?** (Establishes which of the three doors — new notice, document request, already rejected — they actually are, without you naming the categories.)
2. **What did you do first?** (Amazon's own help, a forum, a paid tool, a consultant, nothing yet — this tells you the real competitive set, not the one in the dossier.)
3. **What was the hardest part of understanding what Amazon actually wanted?**
4. **Have you submitted a response yet? What happened after?** (If they've been rejected once: what changed between the first and second attempt, and how did they know what to change?)
5. **Did you consider paying anyone for help? Who, and what stopped you — or what made you decide to pay?** (This is the willingness-to-pay question, asked indirectly instead of "would you pay $249" — a hypothetical price question gets a hypothetical, usually inflated, answer.)
6. **If I showed you something that kept a record of what Amazon asked, what you sent, and what came back — would that have helped? Where?**
7. **What would you need to see before trusting a tool like this with your case?**
8. Only after all of the above: show them the actual product (or a screenshot), and ask **"Does this match what you needed — and is there anything here you wouldn't use, or something missing?"**

### What to listen for, and where it maps

| Signal | Maps to |
|---|---|
| They mention a second or third rejection unprompted | Confirms Door C (already-rejected) is real and findable, not just theoretically well-evidenced |
| They describe drowning in files/emails, not the writing itself | Confirms the case-record value over the drafting value |
| They name a specific price they already almost paid | The only real willingness-to-pay data point this phase can produce |
| They ask "does this send anything to Amazon" or similar before you mention it | Confirms local-first/read-only is a purchase concern, not just a feature |
| They shrug at Q6 ("I guess that's nice") | A warning sign — the differentiator may matter to us and not to them |
| They mention IP, related-account, or identity verification as their actual situation | Direct evidence for whether the new evidence+outreach lane is worth building next, versus staying with document/POA cases |

### What NOT to do

Don't pitch before Q8. Don't quote the $249 price unless they ask directly — and if they do, answer honestly and note their reaction as data, don't negotiate. Don't promise reinstatement, speed, or any outcome (D6). Don't record or store anything about a real seller's case without their explicit consent, and never let their notice text or account details enter any file, prompt, or system outside a direct conversation.

### What to do with the results

After ten conversations, write one page: which door is real, which pain is loudest, whether the $249 price got any reaction at all, and whether the case-record framing landed or fell flat. That page — not another research phase — is what should decide whether Wave 1 (already-rejected seller slice) or Wave 2 (three doors) comes first, per Phase 4 §2 vs §2a.
