The short answer

The product is real and complete for a solo build. Whether it pays is decided by three things that are not code. The work is four layers, and the batches take about two weeks of AI sessions.

33things a seller can do today

The three things: a card must be chargeable (Paddle is the only way to take money from Pakistan; there is no plan C); someone must be sent to the site (the only fast door is the appeal writers and VAs you reach yourself); and a stranger must pay $249 once for software (nobody in this market has yet been seen to). Odds of a first paid Pass within 60 days: 25 to 40%, rising to 40 to 55% if you bring 10 to 20 writers or VAs to live cases.

What makes a seller stay and pay is now checked against real reviews: a named person who answers, no template, no promise, persistence across every round, a record of what was sent. The product already does most of it by design; three moments fall short today (the first minute, the gate, the first paid round), and every fix is already a row in the plan.

Four things found this week: Paddle's rule says a refund window under 14 days fails its review (we promise 7); the checkout has no "I have read the Terms" tick; our code on GitHub is public; and one open question (sign-out scope) was already closed in code.

Who we are against

The tools and firms that sell an appeal

AppealsPro (free letters, a ticker, fake-looking reviews, no deadline from the notice), Mr Jeff (a score and a root cause invented from the notice; its Trustpilot was suspended for fake reviews), SellerForge (needs your Amazon login), a 751-star GitHub "skill" that is an empty shell, Amazon's own assistant (works inside a healthy account), and the human firms (hundreds of reviews naming people; £700 to $2,495; the one-star pattern is silence after paying). Our first minute beats all of them on honesty and speed; our gaps are a one-tap sample, a shorter phone result, and a first button that says "workspace".

What makes a seller stay and pay

From the reviews: the five-star ones praise a named person, being kept informed, speed, persistence across rounds and honesty about chances. The one-star ones are silence, templates, "literally ChatGPT", paid and nothing, and refund trouble. We already refuse every one of those by design; two are invisible and one is not yet real (no named person, no mailbox).

The treasure test

Ten yes/no questions. Today the product scores 2 of 5 after the first free session and 2 of 5 after the first paid round. After the batches: 5 of 5 and 5 of 5, with the two that are yours (your name on the page, a mailbox that answers).

After the first free session, can the seller say yes to…
  1. I know what Amazon is asking for, in my own notice's words, and by when, or that no date is written and where to look. Yes today.
  2. I know what happens to my money. No today. Fixed by the "Your money" line.
  3. I know which records to gather, why each one, and what to do if I cannot get one. Yes today.
  4. It told me one true thing I did not know. No today. Fixed by the scam check's "looked for" list, the invoice arithmetic, "do not resend the same text", "nothing is due tonight unless Account Health shows a date".
  5. I know what it costs, what stays free, and who is behind it. No today. Fixed by the price at the gate and your name on the page.
After the first paid round, can the buyer say yes to…
  1. I sent Amazon a response made only of my own facts, with no template phrases, that pasted cleanly. No today (the "##" marks; the invisible fact lock).
  2. I have the pack and a dated record of exactly what I sent. Yes today.
  3. When Amazon replied, the case told me what the reply means and what changed, without losing my work. Yes today.
  4. When I wrote to support, a named person answered inside the stated window. No today. Yours: the mailbox and your sentence.
  5. If it goes another round I know what to do next, and I know how to get a refund if I want one. Partly today. Fixed by the "what you now have" panel and the refund decision.
