# Disclosure placement plan: what we say where (12 Oct 2026)

**Status: ON HOLD. A plan for the founder to approve, written from his own idea. Nothing in `src/` changed. Nothing here is built until the founder names it.** Checked the same day against two research agents (`2026-10-12-disclosure-pages-research.md`); the corrections are applied in place and listed in §9.

## 0. The founder's idea, in his words (12 Oct 2026)

> We should never look weak. We should only highlight the "don't" and "can't" lines that make a positive impact, like "we don't log in to your Amazon account". The negative parts should sit in the Terms and Privacy pages, which users rarely read but tick as read. This is not fraud; it is the globally recognised way of using these pages. Also use definitions the way the big marketplaces do: "no expiry" on the home page, and in the Terms "no expiry in time, but a case is not tolerated beyond 20 appeals". Everything is written publicly, so anyone who wants to can read it.

## 1. The short answer

- **Yes to the method.** Lead with what the seller keeps control of. Put operating detail, definitions and fair-use rules in the Terms and Privacy. Stop repeating the same "not" line on five pages. The 10 Oct register found the same thing on its own: "three negatives in a row read defensive" (G-99).
- **Three lines cannot leave the point of sale**, however they are worded: Amazon decides the outcome; this is software, not a lawyer or an appeal writer; some cases are not sold. They stay because a buyer who learns them after paying asks for the money back, and because the regulators who police this category treat a hidden material limit as deception no matter what the buyer ticked. Each of the three can be said in a positive sentence, and the plan below does that.
- **The "20 appeals" definition is fine**, as a fair-use clause, because no real case comes near it (the worst documented case was 12 appeals). A definition that nobody hits is a definition; a definition that real buyers hit is a hidden limit. That is the test for every clause in section 4.
- **The result is a site that reads stronger, not weaker, and the same disclosures a careful buyer finds today, in the places a careful buyer looks.**

## 2. The rule we would apply (three tests for every "not" line)

A line goes to the Terms or Privacy page, instead of the page a visitor reads, only if all three hold:

1. **Would it change a reasonable buyer's decision to pay $249?** If yes, it stays at the point of sale (the pricing page, the checkout card, the gate where the price is shown). This is the US FTC's "material" test and the EU/UK "misleading omission" test. The Terms tick does not cure it: regulators look at what the buyer saw, not at what they clicked.
2. **Does a public page say the opposite, or imply it?** If a page says "unlimited" and the Terms say "up to 20", that is a contradiction: a Terms line that contradicts a page is read against us by whoever decides a refund, a chargeback or a complaint, and the FTC's rule is that a disclosure which contradicts a material claim "will not be sufficient". If the page says "every round" and the Terms define "round" and set an anti-abuse rule far above real use, that is a definition. (Corrected 12 Oct: Paddle's review checks that the pages exist, are linked from navigation and the product fits its Acceptable Use Policy, and "automatically approve[s] most domain submissions"; it does not state that it compares the Terms with the pricing page.)
3. **Is it a locked decision?** D6 (the honest-expectations card before purchase, "guarantee" greps to 0, severity gating stated), D8 (the EU-withdrawal consent card and the confirmation email) and the 22 Sep legal-boundaries fixes (not legal advice; where the service stops; nothing is privileged) are locked. They can be reworded; they cannot be moved off the page they protect.

4. **Is it a page element Paddle requires?** (Added 12 Oct.) The Merchant-of-Record sentence in the Terms, the legal name in Terms and Privacy, a refund policy with a window Paddle accepts, and Terms, Refund and Privacy linked from site navigation are fixed by Paddle's approval checklist and are not subject to tests 1 to 3.

Everything else moves.

## 6. What the founder decides

1. **Approve the three tests in section 2** as the standing rule for copy (it becomes a line in `src/content/README.md`'s voice rules and a `lint:copy` note, so a future session cannot move a material line into the Terms).
2. **Approve the eight Terms definitions in section 4** (T1 to T8), or strike any. T2's number (twenty) is the founder's to set; the rule is that it must sit far above any real case.
3. **Approve the rewordings in 5.2 and the removals in 5.4.** Each is one string in `src/content`; the legal-disclosures test (`src/content/__tests__/legalDisclosures.test.ts`) pins the material lines and will be updated in the same commit, never loosened.
4. **Decide where the former "What we will not do" page's lines live now** (G-44 is "don't" since 12 Oct): the proposal is the Terms "Basis of the service" section (already has "We do not log in… do not submit… do not promise") plus the support page's list. No new page.

5. **(Added 12 Oct) The refund window.** Paddle's published seller-policy guidance says a change-of-mind window "must be 14–90 days" and "a sub-14-day refund window ... fail[s] review" (verified); the live site promises 7 days (D8). Keep 7 and ask Paddle in writing, or move to 14 (recommended). F-15 in `docs/PARKED-ITEMS.md`.
6. **(Added 12 Oct) The Terms additions and the checkout tick in §9**, which the research found missing: the Merchant-of-Record sentence, the governing-law split, the changes clause, the conspicuous liability cap, the business-tool line, and "I have read the Terms and the Refund policy" on the consent row.

When approved, this is one batch (copy only, no `src/core`, no API, no Pass change; the consent-row label is one string plus two links), gated as usual, built only when the founder names it ("do the disclosure batch").

## 7. What this plan does not do

- It does not remove a single fact from the site. Every line stays readable on a page a visitor can open.
- It does not touch the D8 consent checkbox or the confirmation email.
- It does not change the refund terms, the price or the severity gate.
- It does not rely on the Terms tick. The Terms are where definitions and operating rules belong; they are not where a buyer's decision gets changed after the fact.

## 9. Research check, 12 Oct 2026 (later the same day)

Two research agents checked this plan against how the best companies do it and against the law (`2026-10-12-disclosure-pages-research.md`). **Corrected in place**

- Test 2's reason: Paddle does not claim to read the Terms against the pricing page; the reader of a contradiction is a refund analyst, a chargeback reviewer or a regulator (§2).
- Test 4 added for Paddle's page requirements (§2).
- T2 rewritten as an anti-abuse rule about one seller's own case; the number is an outer bound and never appears on a public page (§4). No peer checked hides a count behind a public "unlimited" or "every"; FTC v. AT&T ($60M, 2019) is what a numeric cap under an unqualified promise costs once it bites.

- The §5.2 files line keeps "clearing the browser removes them".
- The §5.1 paste-box line becomes "The notice text is not kept."
- §6 gains the refund-window decision (item 5) and the Terms additions (item 6).
