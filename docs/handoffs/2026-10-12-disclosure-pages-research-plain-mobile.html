The short answer

Half of your idea is exactly what the best companies do. The other half is the thing regulators fine. The plan uses the right half, and today's research fixed four details in it.

Right: definitions (what a "case" is, what a "round" is, what a "device" is), operating rules (rate limits, fair use, support hours) and privacy mechanics belong in the Terms and Privacy pages. The tick box is what turns them into a contract.

Not right: anything that would change a buyer's decision to pay cannot live only in the Terms. The US regulator's own words: fine print "may be insufficient", necessary disclosures "should not be relegated to 'terms of use'", and "burying key limitations of the product or service in dense Terms of Service documents" is a named trick. The UK wrote the same into law in 2025. Pakistan's competition regulator says it too: fine-print disclaimers that hide important information "must be avoided", and its fines hit Rs1.007 billion last year.

What the best companies do instead: they put the condition next to the headline, on the same card. "Unlimited file uploads" next to "~5GB max per file". "$0/mo" next to "Mercury is a fintech company, not an FDIC-insured bank". No company we checked hides a number behind a public "unlimited". That is why the plan's three lines stay on the pricing card, and why the "twenty responses" rule becomes an anti-abuse rule instead of a hidden number.

The twenty-response rule becomes: "It is for one seller's own case. Definitions of case, round, device and AI availability go into the Terms.

What the law says, in one table

  • The price and what is includedMust be where you pay: the pricing page, the order summary, the checkout card. The Terms may hold the definitions behind the words, as long as none shrinks what the page promised.
  • A limit that would change the decision to buyAmazon decides; software, not a law firm; cases we refuse; files live on your device; written for Amazon US; not sold in the EU/UK. Must sit next to the claim it qualifies, before the pay button, not behind a link. The Terms may repeat it; the Terms are the record, not the disclosure.
  • An operating ruleRate limits, support hours, device removal, what a refund ends. May live only in the Terms, Privacy, Refund or Support pages, if they are linked from the site and contradict nothing public.
  • A definitionWhat a case, a round, a device is. May live in the Terms if the public word keeps its ordinary meaning. A definition that shrinks it ("every round" meaning "up to twenty") is a limit, and moves up a row. AT&T paid $60 million for "unlimited" qualified only in the fine print.
  • A disclaimer"Not legal advice" must be visible before paying and in the footer. "As is", the liability cap and "nothing is privileged" may live in the Terms, in bold, and bind only if the Terms were really accepted.
  • A privacy factIn the Privacy page in full, and true wherever a page says it in short. "Not stored" beside the paste box is false today (we keep a 7-day fingerprint); it becomes "The notice text is not kept."
  • What Paddle requiresProduct description, pricing, features, Terms/Refund/Privacy linked from navigation, your legal name, a Merchant-of-Record sentence, a refund window Paddle accepts. Fixed by Paddle's checklist, not by the tests above.
Two facts the morning plan did not useYour buyer is a trader, not a consumer, under EU and UK law (a seller buying a tool for a selling business). So for a US buyer the binding rules are the US ones; the EU/UK rules are the right model for wording, not the enforcement route. And the purchase contract is with Paddle under Paddle's own terms (English law), not with you under Pakistani law; our "laws of Pakistan" line can govern only the licence, and should say so.

Most of it holds. Four things changed. These are already corrected in the plan file.

"Amazon decides", "software, not a law firm" and "some cases we do not take" stay before the pay button, reworded to read as confidence.   • Holds: six of the eight Terms definitionsCase and round, device, refund, free-decoder limits, AI availability (beside the AI bullet until the paid AI is on), Amazon US scope, support window.
  • Changed: the "twenty responses" ruleNo company we checked hides a count behind a public "every". It becomes an anti-abuse rule about one seller's own case, with the number only as an outer bound, and a promise to ask before limiting. Never on a public page.
  • Changed: "faster than a law firm" is struckIt compares us to lawyers. That is exactly what the US regulator fined DoNotPay for, and we have no data behind it. The card now says: "Software you run yourself. Not a law firm, and not legal advice. Built around what Amazon's own appeal page asks for."
  • Changed: the files line keeps its warningThe rewrite had dropped "clearing the browser removes them", which is the fact that matters. It is back: "Your files stay on your device, encrypted. Clearing the browser removes them, so make the encrypted backup once the case is live."
  • Changed: the reason behind Test 2The plan said "Paddle reads the whole site". Paddle's own pages say it checks that the pages exist, are linked, and the product fits its rules, and that most sites are approved automatically. The real reader of a contradiction is a refund analyst, a chargeback reviewer or a regulator. The test stays; the reason is corrected.

Two Paddle findings, and two gaps in the Terms

  • Our 7-day refund may fail Paddle's reviewPaddle's published seller-policy guidance (checked by me today): the change-of-mind window "must be 14–90 days"; "a sub-14-day refund window ... fail[s] review". Our live page promises 7 days. Moving to 14 costs nothing measurable and matches Paddle's own 14-day refund-request window. Your call (it is decision D8). F-15 in the parked-items file
  • The checkout has no "I have read the Terms" tickOnly the EU-withdrawal checkbox exists. Under US case law, Terms clauses bind only with clear notice and a clear act. Add "I have read the Terms and the Refund policy" with links to that checkbox's label.
  • The Terms lack the Merchant-of-Record sentence Paddle asks forFor example: "Our order process is conducted by our online reseller Paddle.com. Paddle.com is the Merchant of Record for all our orders."
  • The governing-law line is wrong for the purchaseThe sale is with Paddle under English law. Our "laws of Pakistan" clause should govern the licence only, and say so. Also: the "changes to these terms" line should promise an email to every account holder before a change, and never reduce a Pass already bought; and the liability cap should be in bold with a "does not apply to fraud" carve-out.
